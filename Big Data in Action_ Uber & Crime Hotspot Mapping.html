Where Is the Next Ride? How Uber Turns Location Data into Decisions

By [Your Name] · [Course / Roll No.] · Nov 2026

Every time you open Uber, a system decides in seconds which driver to send, what the price should be, and when you will arrive. Behind that tap sits one of the world’s most location-heavy data platforms.

The story in 4 frames

1. The tap. Meera is late for college and requests a ride. Her phone sends her exact location.

2. The data flows. Her ping, thousands of drivers’ pings and traffic data stream into the platform.

3. The map thinks. The city is split into hexagons. A busy cell near Meera glows darker, so demand beats supply there.

4. The match. The nearest suitable driver is assigned, with a price and an ETA predicted by ML models.

Volumemillions of trips and GPS pings daily
Velocitydriver locations update every few seconds
VarietyGPS, payments, maps, weather, events

The problem

A ride-hailing marketplace must balance drivers and riders by place and by minute. Too few cars in a busy area means long waits and lost riders. Too many means idle drivers. The answer changes block by block, so only big data tools can keep up.

The data pipeline

Phones & GPS→Streaming (Kafka)→Data lake (HDFS)→Processing (Spark, Hive)→ML models→Maps & dashboards
Simplified, based on tools Uber has described publicly (Kafka, Hadoop, Spark, Hive, Presto, Apache Hudi, Michelangelo ML platform).

The geospatial twist: hexagons, not squares

Raw GPS points are useless until grouped into areas. Uber open-sourced H3, a grid that divides the Earth into hexagons at multiple resolutions. Every neighbor of a hexagon is the same distance away, which makes smoothing demand across neighbors fair. Surge pricing and supply balancing work on these cells.

Illustrative demand heatmap (simulated data, not Uber’s). Darker = higher demand. Hotspots: business district, railway station, airport.

Demand has a rhythm

Illustrative hourly demand index (simulated). Orange bars are commute peaks.

From insight to decision

InsightDecision
Demand spikes in a cell and its neighborsDynamic pricing nudges drivers toward it
Predictable hourly and weekly patternsForecast demand, pre-position drivers
Live traffic plus historical tripsMore accurate ETAs and routes
Odd location or payment behaviorFraud and safety flags

Key takeaways

  • Uber’s edge is turning streaming location data into real-time decisions.
  • Gridding space with H3 makes demand measurable and comparable.
  • Challenges: location privacy, GPS errors in dense areas, and fairness of dynamic pricing.

Use Case 2 · Geospatial Analytics (QGIS)

Where Does Crime Cluster? Mapping Hotspots with Geospatial Big Data

By [Your Name] · [Course / Roll No.] · Nov 2026

A city records thousands of incidents every month, but a spreadsheet of addresses cannot tell you where to act. Turn the same rows into a map, and patterns appear that no table can show.

The story in 4 frames

1. Reports pile up. Ravi, a city analyst, receives 100,000 incident records: type, time and an address.

2. Addresses become points. Geocoding converts each address into latitude and longitude on the map.

3. A hotspot appears. Thousands of dots become a heat surface. A small area shows far more incidents than the rest.

4. Action follows data. Patrols, better street lighting and community programs are focused where the map says they matter.

Why this is a big data problem

Cities log incidents for years across many systems, each with messy addresses, different time formats and missing coordinates. Managing, cleaning and joining this data comes first. Mapping it comes second. Crime also concentrates: studies of urban policing repeatedly find that a small share of streets accounts for a large share of incidents, which is exactly why hotspot maps are useful. Agencies such as Chicago and New York police publish open datasets, and Delhi Police has used a crime mapping platform (CMAPS) for similar analysis.

When does it happen?

Illustrative hourly incident index (simulated). Orange bars mark the late-evening peak. Time filters like this are ideal in a Power BI or Tableau dashboard beside the map.

My QGIS workflow

  1. Load: add the incident CSV as a Delimited Text layer using longitude and latitude (EPSG:4326).
  2. Clean: remove rows with missing or out-of-city coordinates, and standardize date and time fields.
  3. Reproject: convert to a metric CRS (such as UTM) so cell sizes and distances are accurate.
  4. Aggregate: create a grid, then use Count Points in Polygon to get incidents per cell.
  5. Visualize: style with graduated colors, and try Heatmap (Kernel Density Estimation) for a smooth surface.
  6. Share: export the grid table to Power BI or Tableau for hour, day and crime-type filters.

From insight to decision

InsightDecision
Persistent hotspot around a market and bus standTargeted patrols and visible policing
Incidents peak late eveningShift staffing and lighting schedules
Dark, poorly lit lanes score highPrioritize street lighting repairs

Handle with care

  • Biased data: more patrols mean more recorded incidents, which can create a feedback loop. Maps show reports, not the full truth.
  • Grid size matters: changing cell size can change the hotspot (the Modifiable Areal Unit Problem), so test several sizes.
  • Privacy and fairness: avoid targeting individuals or neighborhoods unfairly, and show uncertainty.

Key takeaways

  • Geocoding and aggregation turn address lists into decisions.
  • QGIS handles the spatial work, and Power BI or Tableau adds time-based exploration.
  • Good analysts show the map and its limits.
